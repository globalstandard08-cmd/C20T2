READING PASSAGE 3

Invasion of the Robot Umpires

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 27–40, which are based on Reading Passage 3 below.

AA few years ago, Fred DeJesus from Brooklyn, New York became the first umpire in a minor league baseball game to use something called the Automated Ball-Strike System (ABS), often referred to as the 'robot umpire'. Instead of making all judgments himself about a strike, DeJesus had his decisions confirmed through an earpiece, connected to a camera-based tracking system. The system looked like a large black box with one glowing green light; it was mounted above the pitch stand.

BMajor League Baseball (MLB), which had commissioned the system, wanted human umpires to announce the calls, just as they would have done in the past. When the first pitch came in, a recorded voice told DeJesus whether it was a strike or ball, and he repeated it out loud.

CPreviously, a strike was a judgment call made by the umpire. Even if the batter did not swing, a ball that passed through the 'strike zone' — an imaginary rectangle about seventeen inches wide, stretching from the batter's knees to the middle of their chest — counted as a strike. During that first game, when DeJesus announced each call, there was no shouting or argument from players. No one said a word.

DFor over a century, the human element has been central to the game—arguments between managers and umpires have been part of baseball's character. The rules have evolved gradually. Today, everyone knows that you may express disagreement, but you must never insult anyone or touch an umpire. That is not acceptable. With the robot system in place, however, arguments disappeared.

EDuring the first season of testing, players complained about some unusual calls. In response, MLB decided to adjust the dimensions of the zone, and by the following year, most agreed that the system was consistently accurate. MLB says the technology is over 99% accurate, precise to within a fraction of an inch. "It removes controversy and makes the game fairer," says Robert Manfred, who is Commissioner for MLB. But the question remains whether controversy is something that should be removed — or whether it is simply part of what makes the game what it is.

FA human, though, brings something a machine cannot. As one former minor league manager observed, the system may be accurate but it lacks empathy and judgment. "An umpire learns a pitcher's rhythm," he noted. Some pitchers have argued that, compared to a human umpire, the system calls too many strikes at the edges of the zone. An umpire will sometimes give a pitcher the benefit of the doubt on a close call. That human touch is gone.

GThe person leading the project for MLB is Morgan Sword, the league's Head of On-Field Operations. According to Sword, ABS is part of a wider effort to make baseball more engaging to younger fans, who have been turning away from traditional sports. He explains that the system was developed following feedback from fans that games were taking too long. The data shows that accurate calls speed up play. This is something that has not changed since the 1960s, when the average game also moved faster — then, the pitch clock was introduced to keep things moving.

HSword's team looked at many possible changes. Some rules were considered — from changing the size of the strike zone to changing the shape of the ball itself. But while all those ideas were rejected, ABS was seen as the one solution that everyone could accept. According to Sword, once the technology is fully reliable, it could be adapted in future. "It might become a square zone, or different shapes for different stadiums. Over time, the rules themselves could evolve because we no longer depend on human consistency."

I"In other sports, technology has taken over certain decisions," says sports historian Dr Elena Marquez. "Tennis uses it for line calls. Cricket has it for lbw decisions. In those sports, however, the technology only confirms what is clearly measurable. The strike zone is not like that — it depends on the batter's height and stance, which changes every time. Historically, a certain amount of human judgment has always been valued and respected."

JI spoke with Dr Alva Noë, a philosopher who has written extensively on human versus machine decision-making. "It is remarkable how quickly we accept these things," he observed. "We treat a computer's decision as final, but that doesn't make it right. Baseball is not just about getting every call exactly perfect; it is about the drama, the debate, and the shared experience of watching the game together."
